Rooftops that breathe: the return of the living roof

ALong before the term "green infrastructure" existed, people were already living under planted roofs. In Scandinavia, sod roofs — layers of birch bark topped with turf — have sheltered farmhouses for many centuries, prized because the living layer provided natural insulation through freezing winters. The modern green roof, however, is a more engineered affair: a waterproof membrane, a root barrier, a drainage layer and a growing medium, all designed to keep a building dry while a garden thrives above it.

BGermany is widely regarded as the leader in contemporary adoption, with decades of supportive building codes and an estimated hundred million square metres of planted roofs already installed. Designers there distinguish between two broad families. Extensive green roofs use a shallow growing medium, are planted with hardy sedums and grasses, and need very little maintenance once established. Intensive green roofs are closer to true rooftop parks, with deeper soil, larger plants and correspondingly greater structural demands.

CThe case that cities make for living roofs rests, above all, on stormwater. A conventional roof sheds nearly all the rain that lands on it within minutes, sending sudden surges into pipes built for a smaller, drier city. A planted roof behaves more like a sponge: its vegetation and substrate capture a large share of each rainfall and release it slowly, easing the pressure on ageing drainage systems. The benefits do not stop there. In summer, the plants cool the air around them through evapotranspiration, and studies repeatedly show that buildings beneath living roofs spend noticeably less on summer cooling.

DRegulation has begun to follow the evidence. Toronto became the first city in North America to pass a bylaw requiring green roofs on most large new developments, and several European and Asian cities have introduced comparable rules or generous subsidies. Such mandates remain the exception rather than the norm, and most construction worldwide still proceeds with conventional roofing.

EWhat, then, holds the living roof back? The answer is rarely technical. Membranes and drainage products are mature, and structural solutions exist for most buildings. The obstacle is financial: the initial cost of a green roof can be two to three times that of a standard one, and the savings it generates — lower energy bills, longer membrane life, reduced flood damage — accrue slowly and often to someone other than the developer who pays. Until valuation methods catch up with these distributed benefits, the living roof will likely spread the way it has so far: steadily, quietly, one persuaded rooftop at a time.
